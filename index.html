A note from the builder

The First Time I Managed People

Nobody handed me a manual. There wasn’t exactly training, I just figured it out as I went. I read books, I looked things up online, I did what you do.

But what I actually wanted was someone to talk to who wasn’t my own manager, because I didn’t want to look incompetent in front of them. Someone I could get into the actual specifics with.

▾

And specifics take detail. They take nuance. That’s the part that’s genuinely boring to bring to your own manager: they’ve got a lot on their plate, and most of the time they don’t need to hear it, and honestly shouldn’t have to.

What I needed was a confidant. Someone I could lay out the whole situation for, in as much detail as it actually took, and just get a gut check from. Not a verdict, not a script, just someone to think out loud with before I had to go handle it for real.

That’s the gap Mend is built to fill. Somewhere to work through the specifics, off the record, before the hard conversation, instead of guessing your way through it alone.

Why it matters

Getting it wrong costs more than the conversation.

Nobody trained you to handle conflict between the people you manage. There's no feedback loop for how you're doing it — so the instinct is to avoid it, improvise it, or escalate it too early. Any of those costs the team more than the five minutes it would've taken to get it right.

For you
Confidence without formal training, and full autonomy over what you do with what you work out.
For the team
Unresolved tension doesn't stay contained — it leaks into the room and drags on everyone's focus.

The Mend Approach to Workplace Friction

Stated simply, we value people over:

  • Structural clarity over litigating blame. Workplace friction stems from colliding incentives, competing KPIs, and overloaded schedules, not malice or "bad people." We solve the system, not the personality.
  • Radical privacy over managerial surveillance. Honest resolution cannot survive an audience. People only own their part when their words cannot be weaponized on a performance review or an HR docket.
  • Personal agency over forced corporate harmony. We never demand apologies, preach toxic positivity, or force coworkers to become friends. Setting clear, functional operational boundaries is just as valid.
  • Decoupled consent over performative truces. Concessions made under live social pressure fall apart the moment people leave the room. True agreements are evaluated and signed alone, with zero coercion and no audience.
  • Dissolving sutures over permanent dossiers. The process exists purely to stabilize the dynamic while it repairs. Once alignment holds, the record dissolves completely.

What We Mean by “Solving the System”

When an argument feels personal, the instinct is to diagnose the other person as difficult, incompetent, or toxic. But in high-velocity teams, conflict almost always comes down to structural design:

  • Colliding Incentives. Engineering is penalized for regressions and system instability, while Product is measured on delivery speed. Both are doing their jobs, which puts their day-to-day workflows on a direct collision course.
  • Unclear Swimlanes & SLAs. Vague ownership, ambiguous handoffs, and misaligned turnaround expectations create friction that masquerades as interpersonal hostility.
  • Operational Fixes. Instead of trying to fix personalities, we fix the machine, aligning delivery criteria, defining explicit handoff boundaries, and removing structural mismatches so both people can succeed without friction.

How Thimble Actually Works

Not a generic chatbot wearing a mascot. Here's what's actually running underneath.

  • A purpose-built system prompt, not a persona bolted onto a general assistant. Thimble's entire conduct — short, spoken-style turns instead of essays, never taking either side, pushing back on a plan that's vague or likely to blindside someone — comes from a system prompt written and iterated specifically for workplace conflict conversations, not a generic "be helpful" instruction.
  • Harassment & safety floor recognition. Every turn, in every phase, gets continuously screened against a fixed set of categories — harassment or discrimination based on a protected characteristic, threats, illegal activity, immediate safety risk, retaliation for something someone had a legitimate right to do. This isn't a keyword filter: it re-weighs the whole conversation as new detail comes in, and a vague warning or a sudden cold shoulder can meet the floor just as much as an explicit threat.
  • Deterministic rules on top of the model, not just a vibe. Once the floor is met, what happens next isn't up for interpretation: the session stops coaching toward a plan, the person is told plainly and never silently, and Mend sends nothing to HR without their explicit, informed yes — no silent reports, ever.
  • A second opinion on genuinely ambiguous cases. When a disclosure is a real toss-up, not just sensitive, Thimble doesn't settle it alone on a single pass — it runs an any-of-k self-consistency check (several independent reads voting) before the floor decision is finalized, instead of trusting one read of one conversation.
