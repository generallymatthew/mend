The Third Way to Be Professional

Earlier in my career, whenever I ran into tension with a coworker, I did what almost everyone does: I asked my friends.

Because they cared about me, they validated me. They told me I was right, that the other person was being unreasonable, and that I didn’t deserve it. It felt good in the moment. But they didn’t tell me what I actually needed to hear: my own part in the dynamic.

▾

Armed with that validation, I made reactive, defensive choices that set my career back years.

People pretend workplace dynamics are common sense. They aren’t. They’re complicated, emotionally charged, and rarely taught.

Worse, corporate culture tells us the only way to be “professional” is to pretend our humanity doesn’t exist: check your feelings at the door, smile through misaligned expectations, and treat emotional friction like an HR malfunction. But suppressing how you feel doesn’t make you professional, it just drives the frustration into quiet avoidance and backchannel gossip.

We built Mend around a third option: being professionally human.

You don’t have to turn into an emotionless corporate cog, and you don’t have to sabotage your reputation either. Mend is the private, neutral mirror I wish I had back then: a place to drop the filter, be messy and honest off the record, figure out what’s actually happening beneath the surface, and handle conflict like a grown-up before walking into the room.

The Cost of the Usual Options

Most conflict at work isn't personal, it's structural: unclear roles, competing incentives, bad process. Treating it as a character problem instead of a structural one is why it almost never gets fixed.

Say Nothing (Avoidance)The default choice. Tension festers, code reviews get delayed, and team collaboration quietly grinds to a halt.
Vent to a CoworkerFeels cathartic for an hour, but fixes nothing structurally and drags uninvolved teammates into backchannel politics.
Escalate to HR or ManagementCreates an adversarial paper trail for an issue that was simply two people out of sync, turning normal friction into a formal performance risk.
Ask the AI App on Your PhoneA general-purpose chatbot wasn't built for this. It has no grounding in conflict resolution methodology and no way to reliably tell a genuine misunderstanding apart from conduct that's actually harassment, so it can just as easily talk you out of raising something serious.

The Mend Manifesto

We value mending relationships over:

  • Structural clarity over litigating blame. Workplace friction stems from colliding incentives, competing KPIs, and overloaded schedules, not malice or "bad people." We solve the system, not the personality.
  • Radical privacy over managerial surveillance. Honest resolution cannot survive an audience. People only own their part when their words cannot be weaponized on a performance review or an HR docket.
  • Personal agency over forced corporate harmony. We never demand apologies, preach toxic positivity, or force coworkers to become friends. Setting clear, functional operational boundaries is just as valid.
  • Decoupled consent over performative truces. Concessions made under live social pressure fall apart the moment people leave the room. True agreements are evaluated and signed alone, with zero coercion and no audience.
  • Dissolving sutures over permanent dossiers. The process exists purely to stabilize the dynamic while it repairs. Once alignment holds, the record dissolves completely.

What We Mean by “Solving the System”

When an argument feels personal, the instinct is to diagnose the other person as difficult, incompetent, or toxic. But in high-velocity teams, conflict almost always comes down to structural design:

  • Colliding Incentives. Engineering is penalized for regressions and system instability, while Product is measured on delivery speed. Both are doing their jobs, which puts their day-to-day workflows on a direct collision course.
  • Unclear Swimlanes & SLAs. Vague ownership, ambiguous handoffs, and misaligned turnaround expectations create friction that masquerades as interpersonal hostility.
  • Operational Fixes. Instead of trying to fix personalities, we fix the machine, aligning delivery criteria, defining explicit handoff boundaries, and removing structural mismatches so both people can succeed without friction.
