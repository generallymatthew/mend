What actually changed

Reading people at work used to be something you built up almost by accident, through sheer volume of exposure. A few things have quietly cut that off.

Remote work
Fewer low-stakes reps
Hallway chat, the five minutes before a meeting starts, watching someone's face while they talk: that's where you used to calibrate, for free, on easy stakes. Most of that didn't move to video calls. It just stopped happening. What's left is a thinner stream of interactions, and each one now matters more because you have less practice to fall back on.
AI agents
Fewer people between you and the room
A lot of what used to buffer you, a PM drafting the update, a manager pressure-testing your message before it reached leadership, is now done by an agent instead of a person. The work still gets done, but the judgment call about how it'll land doesn't come with it. That call now lands on you, directly, with no one checking it first.
AI production
Everyone has an opinion now
When a draft, a design, or a deck takes thirty seconds to produce instead of a week, the output stops speaking for itself and every stakeholder in the room feels entitled to weigh in. The job shifts from defending hours of work to defending a judgment call, and that call only holds up if you can tie it to what the people using it actually need, not just your own preference.

Put together: fewer reps to learn from, no one left to catch it when you misjudge something, and more people than ever with a stake in whether your read was right. That combination is new, and it's why this feels harder even though nothing about you changed.

What Mend does about it

Before a message goes out, or after an exchange that didn't sit right, Mend gives you the read you used to get from a person who had your back: is this actually a problem, or does it just feel like one because you don't have the data to tell the difference anymore.

Ways to use it

Pressure-test a message
Before it goes to someone who doesn't know you well, run it past Mend first. Not a tone-softener, a read on how it's actually likely to land.
Make sense of a reaction
Something landed wrong and you're not sure why. Walk through it and figure out whether it was real, or whether you're reading too much into too little data.
Rehearse the conversation
For the conversation you're about to have with someone you don't get regular face time with anymore. Say it out loud first, somewhere private.

Why not just ask a coworker, or your own chatbot

Asking a coworker costs you something: it signals you weren't sure, and in a flatter org there may not be anyone with the standing to catch it for you anyway. A general chatbot has the opposite problem, it only ever hears your side and is built to agree with you, so it can't tell you when you're wrong.
