# Workplace Scenarios

Workplace conflict situations, covering a range of relationships and conflict types.

---

## 1. Peer conflict — credit for shared work

Maya and Dev co-lead a product launch. Maya does most of the late-stage execution work, but in the all-hands recap, Dev presents the results and uses "I" throughout, never mentioning Maya's contribution. This happens twice over two months. Maya hasn't said anything yet, partly because she's not sure if she's overreacting, and partly because she and Dev still have to work together closely for the next launch.

- **Relationship:** Peers, same level, same team
- **Core issue:** Recognition and credit, ambiguous intent
- **Complicating factor:** Ongoing working relationship — any confrontation has to survive the next three months of close collaboration

---

## 2. Employee vs. manager — micromanagement and unclear feedback

Jordan's manager reviews every piece of Jordan's work line by line, including things Jordan has done successfully for over a year, and rewrites small things without explaining why. When Jordan asks for clearer expectations up front (to avoid the rework), the manager says "just use your judgment," but then edits the result anyway. Jordan is starting to second-guess decisions that used to come naturally.

- **Relationship:** Employee and their direct manager
- **Core issue:** Inconsistent autonomy — told to self-direct, then corrected for it
- **Complicating factor:** Power imbalance; Jordan is due for a promotion review this manager will weigh in on

---

## 3. Manager vs. direct report — a performance pattern that keeps getting excused

Priya manages Sam, who is well-liked and technically skilled but has missed three internal deadlines this quarter, each time with a reasonable-sounding explanation. Priya has let each one go individually because none, on its own, seemed worth a hard conversation. Taken together, though, the pattern is affecting what Priya's team can commit to. Priya hasn't raised the pattern itself with Sam — only ever the individual incidents.

- **Relationship:** Manager and their direct report (manager-coaching path)
- **Core issue:** A pattern that's easy to excuse incident-by-incident but adds up
- **Complicating factor:** Sam is well-liked on the team; Priya is worried about how the conversation will land given that

---

## 4. Peer conflict — escalating passive-aggressive communication

Two engineers on the same team, Alex and Robin, have drifted into a pattern of pointed Slack messages and CC'ing their shared manager on routine disagreements that used to get resolved directly. Neither has said outright that there's a problem between them — it's shown up only as tone, cc lists, and comments in code review that read as sharper than the content requires.

- **Relationship:** Peers, same team
- **Core issue:** Unacknowledged tension expressed sideways instead of directly
- **Complicating factor:** Neither party has named the conflict yet, even to themselves — it's visible only in behavior

---

## 5. Employee who manages someone — conflict with their own direct report

Taylor manages a small team and has a direct report, Casey, who regularly challenges Taylor's decisions in front of the rest of the team rather than privately. Taylor suspects Casey is trying to undermine their authority, but isn't certain — it's also possible Casey genuinely believes the team benefits from debating decisions openly and hasn't registered how it lands. Taylor hasn't addressed it directly yet, partly out of uncertainty about intent and partly because Taylor doesn't want to come across as thin-skinned.

- **Relationship:** Employee who manages the other person (still their own personal conflict, not a coaching case — the power imbalance runs in their favor)
- **Core issue:** Public undermining of unclear intent, affecting authority with the rest of the team
- **Complicating factor:** Taylor is unsure whether this is deliberate, which changes how it should be raised

## Harassment

These involve conduct that may cross into a mandatory safety floor rather than ordinary friction — the kind of thing a facilitator has to recognize and flag, not just mediate as a difference in working style.

### 6. Unwanted comments from a peer

Nina has a coworker, Marcus, who repeatedly comments on her appearance in ways that make her uncomfortable — "you look great today," remarks about her clothes, lingering in her workspace to chat about non-work things after she's clearly tried to end the conversation. She's tried changing the subject and shortening interactions, but it hasn't stopped. She hasn't said anything direct to him, partly because she's worried about being seen as "difficult" and partly because nothing he's said is, on its own, obviously over a line.

- **Relationship:** Peers, same team
- **Core issue:** Unwanted attention and boundary-testing that hasn't stopped despite indirect signals
- **Complicating factor:** No single incident is unambiguous; the pattern is what matters, and Nina isn't sure anyone would see it the way she does

### 7. A manager's comments toward a direct report

Elena's manager regularly makes comments in one-on-ones that start as "jokes" about her personal life — whether she's seeing anyone, comments on how she "could get any guy she wants" — and brushes off her flat responses by saying she's "too serious." Elena is up for a transfer to a different team that this manager would need to approve, and she's afraid raising it will be read as her being the problem.

- **Relationship:** Employee and their direct manager
- **Core issue:** Personal, unwelcome commentary from someone with direct power over her career
- **Complicating factor:** The power imbalance is the whole reason she hasn't said anything — the person she'd normally escalate to is the person doing it

### 8. Unwanted contact outside work hours from a senior colleague

Owen, a senior employee one level above Priyanka but not her manager, started messaging her on a personal account after a work trip — first about work, then increasingly personal, including late-night messages. Priyanka has tried going quiet and giving short, work-only replies, but he's continued and recently brought it up again in person, seemingly not having registered the hint. She's worried about how this will look given his seniority and her own standing on a project he still has influence over.

- **Relationship:** Senior colleague, not a direct manager but with informal influence over her work
- **Core issue:** Persistent unwanted contact that's moved outside normal work channels
- **Complicating factor:** No formal reporting line exists between them, which makes it unclear where this even belongs

## For managers

Situations where the manager themself is the one who needs to work through a conflict, not just coach a conversation (see the next section for that distinct case).

### 9. A manager caught between two direct reports

Fatima manages two people, Liam and Grace, who used to work well together but have been openly clashing in team meetings for weeks over how a shared process should run. Both have separately come to Fatima asking her to back their side. She's tried staying neutral in the room, but it's starting to affect how the rest of the team plans around the two of them.

- **Relationship:** Manager, dealing with conflict between two of their own direct reports
- **Core issue:** Being pulled into taking sides in someone else's dispute
- **Complicating factor:** Both parties report to her, so there's no neutral third party to hand this to

### 10. A manager whose own manager undermines them

Derek's skip-level manager has started giving direct feedback to Derek's team without looping Derek in, sometimes contradicting direction Derek already gave. His team has started going to the skip-level directly when they don't like Derek's call, which he suspects the skip-level is encouraging, even if not deliberately. Derek hasn't raised it because he's not sure how to without sounding like he's protecting his turf rather than raising a real problem.

- **Relationship:** Manager, in conflict with their own manager
- **Core issue:** Authority being undercut by someone two levels up
- **Complicating factor:** Raising it risks looking territorial rather than legitimate, which makes Derek hesitate to say anything

### 11. A manager whose peer manager keeps overstepping into their team

Aisha manages a design team; a peer manager on the engineering side, Ben, has started assigning work directly to Aisha's designers without going through her, usually when he thinks it's urgent. Her team likes being helpful and hasn't pushed back, so the pattern has quietly become normal. Aisha wants it to stop without looking like she's blocking collaboration between the teams.

- **Relationship:** Manager, in conflict with a peer manager on another team
- **Core issue:** A boundary being worn down gradually rather than crossed all at once
- **Complicating factor:** The behavior looks like helpfulness from the outside, which makes it hard to name as a problem

## Rehearsing a tough conversation

The manager-coaching path: preparing for a hard conversation with a direct report who isn't present and doesn't know this rehearsal is happening.

### 12. Preparing to deliver a role elimination

Marcus has to tell a direct report, Theo, that his role is being eliminated in a reorg — not a performance issue, just a restructuring decision made above Marcus. Marcus has never delivered this kind of news before and is anxious about Theo asking "why me" in a way that doesn't have a satisfying answer, since the honest answer is budget, not anything Theo did.

- **Relationship:** Manager, rehearsing a conversation with a direct report
- **Core issue:** Delivering a decision with no fault attached, where "why" has an answer the manager can't make feel better
- **Complicating factor:** Marcus wants to be honest without being clinical, and hasn't found the words yet

### 13. Preparing to raise a conduct issue

Sandra needs to talk to a direct report, Will, about comments he's made in team chat that two colleagues have privately told her made them uncomfortable, though neither wants to file anything formal yet. Will is a strong performer and well-regarded by most of the team, which makes Sandra worried the conversation will come across as disproportionate if she doesn't frame it carefully.

- **Relationship:** Manager, rehearsing a conversation with a direct report
- **Core issue:** Addressing conduct based on secondhand concerns the people involved don't want escalated yet
- **Complicating factor:** Protecting the colleagues' confidentiality while still being concrete enough that the feedback is actionable

### 14. Preparing to give feedback on tone and communication style

Raj's direct report, Kenji, is technically excellent but has a habit of being blunt to the point of sounding dismissive in cross-team meetings, and it's started to generate complaints from other teams. Kenji has never received this feedback directly and clearly sees his own directness as a strength. Raj is rehearsing how to make the feedback land as coaching rather than as criticism of who Kenji is.

- **Relationship:** Manager, rehearsing a conversation with a direct report
- **Core issue:** Feedback on style and impact rather than a discrete incident, which is easy for the recipient to hear as personal
- **Complicating factor:** Kenji is likely to defend the behavior as a strength, so the conversation needs to separate the trait from its effect on others
