The Mend Manifesto

We solve the system, not the personality

Workplace friction stems from colliding incentives, competing KPIs, and overloaded schedules, not malice or "bad people." Everything below is the operating philosophy behind every session Thimble runs, not a mission-statement paragraph bolted on after the fact.

The problem, and what Mend actually does about it

Working through peer tension isn't an emergency or a personal failing. It's proactive team hygiene that keeps projects shipping, and the value lands differently depending on which side of the relationship you're on.

What you get as an employee
Zero career risk
Resolving friction peer-to-peer prevents you or your coworker from getting tagged as "difficult" or "culture risks" on a management radar.
Drop the defenses
Without managers or HR watching, you don't have to posture or defend your ego. You can own your part and fix the dynamic with your dignity intact.
Full autonomy
You decide what actions to take. If you only want to process your thoughts or rehearse solo, you never have to invite anyone else.
Why HR & leadership support this
Protects sprint velocity
Unresolved tension leaks into the entire room, pulling surrounding teammates into toxic backchannels and lost focus.
Reclaims 2.8 hours a week
Cuts the 2.8 hours per employee lost each week to circular avoidance, re-reading Slack messages, and context switching.
Frees HR for high-value strategy
Shields People Ops and managers from spending 3–5 hours a week acting as informal arbiters for everyday communication bumps.

What we value over

Five commitments, each one a deliberate trade against a more conventional, more comfortable alternative.

Structural clarity over litigating blame
Workplace friction stems from colliding incentives, competing KPIs, and overloaded schedules, not malice or "bad people." We solve the system, not the personality.
Radical privacy over managerial surveillance
Honest resolution cannot survive an audience. People only own their part when their words cannot be weaponized on a performance review or an HR docket.
Personal agency over forced corporate harmony
We never demand apologies, preach toxic positivity, or force coworkers to become friends. Setting clear, functional operational boundaries is just as valid.
Decoupled consent over performative truces
Concessions made under live social pressure fall apart the moment people leave the room. True agreements are evaluated and signed alone, with zero coercion and no audience.
Dissolving sutures over permanent dossiers
The process exists purely to stabilize the dynamic while it repairs. Once alignment holds, the record dissolves completely.

What we mean by "solving the system"

When an argument feels personal, the instinct is to diagnose the other person as difficult, incompetent, or toxic. In high-velocity teams, conflict almost always comes down to structural design instead.

Colliding incentives
Engineering is penalized for regressions and system instability, while Product is measured on delivery speed. Both are doing their jobs, which puts their day-to-day workflows on a direct collision course.
Unclear swimlanes & SLAs
Vague ownership, ambiguous handoffs, and misaligned turnaround expectations create friction that masquerades as interpersonal hostility.
Operational fixes
Instead of trying to fix personalities, we fix the machine: aligning delivery criteria, defining explicit handoff boundaries, and removing structural mismatches so both people can succeed without friction.

Why the usual options don't fix it

Most conflict at work isn't personal, it's structural. Treating it as a character problem instead of a structural one is why it almost never gets fixed.

Say nothing (avoidance)The default choice. Tension festers, code reviews get delayed, and team collaboration quietly grinds to a halt.
Vent to a coworkerFeels cathartic for an hour, but fixes nothing structurally and drags uninvolved teammates into backchannel politics.
Escalate to HR or managementCreates an adversarial paper trail for an issue that was simply two people out of sync, turning normal friction into a formal performance risk.
Resolve with MendTalk it through privately with a facilitator trained on current conflict-resolution methodology and specifically on the distinction between a misunderstanding and harassment, so it knows when to help you work things out and when to automatically route to HR instead.
Resolving conflict directly is a sign of maturity, not a failure. The people who engage with it should be seen as the adults in the room, not as the source of the problem.

Where advocacy stops

Thimble is not neutral toward the company; it's an advocate for both employees' own interests. That stance has exactly one limit.

+Where a real power imbalance exists, seniority, tenure, a reporting relationship, the more vulnerable party is proactively advised on how to protect themselves, not just how to smooth things over.
–The advocacy stance yields the moment an employee's own conduct meets the mandatory floor. Being "on your side" was never meant to mean illegal conduct gets a pass.
