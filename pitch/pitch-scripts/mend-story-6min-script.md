# Mend: a 6-minute story

*Spoken script, roughly 820 words, paced for ~6 minutes. Not a pitch, no ask, no numbers.*

---

Three weeks ago I was trying to get through a work call while my sons, twelve and thirteen, were in the kitchen having what was apparently the trial of the century over a phone charger. Who took it. Who had it first. Whose fault it was that neither of them could charge their phone. This had been going on, in one form or another, for about forty minutes.

I was playing around with AI at the time, looking for something to build. And somewhere in that forty minutes I had the thought: what if I just built something that listens to this instead of me. Something that hears the whole boring charger saga, works out what's actually going on, and hands me a solution, so I can get back to my call.

So I built a quick prototype. Fed it the argument. And it did something I didn't expect. It didn't just settle the charger thing. It noticed that the charger wasn't really the fight. The actual fight was that one of them felt like he never got asked first for anything, and the charger was just today's proof of it.

That's when it clicked for me that this wasn't just a parenting hack. If an AI could sit inside two twelve year olds' stupid argument and find the real thing underneath it, there was no reason that couldn't work somewhere with real stakes. Like a workplace.

Here's the thing about workplace conflict: by the time it reaches HR, it's already a disaster. Two people who've been building resentment for months, finally boiling over in a meeting room with a manager who has twenty minutes and no training for this. And everyone in that room already knows the paper trail starts the second they walk in. Whatever gets said can end up in a file. So people don't say the real thing. They say the safe thing. And the actual problem never gets looked at.

I don't think people are usually the problem. I think most workplace conflict is a structural problem wearing a person's face. Someone's getting pulled between two managers with competing priorities, and it looks like an attitude problem. Someone's overloaded and quietly dropping balls, and it looks like they don't care. The fight people have is almost never the fight that's actually happening.

So that's what I started building for real. Mend. The idea is simple: give someone a space to talk it out, privately, with an AI that's actually trying to understand what's happening underneath, not file a report. No transcript. No record a manager or HR can pull up later. The conversation exists while it's happening and then it's gone, the same way a conversation with a trusted friend is gone once you've had it.

And that "no paper trail" part isn't a limitation. It's the whole point. The reason this hasn't already been built by Microsoft or Slack or Workday is that their entire business model runs on keeping records. Employees know that. So no employee is ever going to fully open up inside a tool built by the same company that runs their compliance logs. Mend only works because it's built by nobody those companies, which means it has to earn trust architecturally, not with a privacy policy nobody reads.

I've spent the last few months actually building this. Not a deck, a real thing. A version where an employee can talk through a conflict and get coached on how to raise it themselves. A version for managers, so they can work a case between two people on their team without taking sides, and it remembers where things stand so they're not starting from zero every time. A live, two-person conversation where the AI sits in the middle of an actual hard conversation between two people, in real time, and knows when to step in.

And building it taught me the same lesson over and over. Every time I thought I'd nailed the hard part, testing it for real against a real conversation found something I'd missed. A line that sounded like fake reassurance. A question that got asked twice because the AI wasn't actually listening to what had already been said. Small things, but they're the difference between something that feels like it understands you and something that feels like a form with a nicer voice.

Where this goes next isn't just "solve office conflict." It's a broader bet that a lot of the friction we live with, at work, on teams, probably even in families, isn't a people problem. It's institutions, or just the shape of how we're organized, putting pressure on people until it comes out sideways. If you can get underneath that, even once, you've done something most HR departments, most managers, and most of us as parents never actually get to.

I still haven't fully solved the charger thing, by the way. But I know what I'm going to build next.
