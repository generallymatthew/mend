The Third Way to Be Professional

Earlier in my career, whenever I ran into tension with a coworker, I did what almost everyone does: I asked my friends.

Because they cared about me, they validated me. They told me I was right, that the other person was being unreasonable, and that I didn’t deserve it. It felt good in the moment. But they didn’t tell me what I actually needed to hear: my own part in the dynamic.

▾

Armed with that validation, I made reactive, defensive choices that set my career back years.

People pretend workplace dynamics are common sense. They aren’t. They’re complicated, emotionally charged, and rarely taught.

Worse, corporate culture tells us the only way to be “professional” is to pretend our humanity doesn’t exist: check your feelings at the door, smile through misaligned expectations, and treat emotional friction like an HR malfunction. But suppressing how you feel doesn’t make you professional, it just drives the frustration into quiet avoidance and backchannel gossip.

We built Mend around a third option: being professionally human.

You don’t have to turn into an emotionless corporate cog, and you don’t have to sabotage your reputation either. Mend is the private, neutral mirror I wish I had back then: a place to drop the filter, be messy and honest off the record, figure out what’s actually happening beneath the surface, and handle conflict like a grown-up before walking into the room.

What employees actually do today after they have been involved in a conflict at work

  • Do nothing and overthink it. Resolves nothing.
  • Back-channel to friendly teammates for validation. Resolves nothing, and often spreads the conflict to people who weren't part of it.
  • Take it to a manager or HR. This resolves something, but the resolution is out of the employee's hands, with real career risk if it lands the wrong way.

Why not just use a general-purpose AI chatbot?

A reasonable question, since every employee already has one in their pocket. The honest answer: a general chatbot wasn't built for this. It has no grounding in conflict-resolution methodology, and no reliable way to tell a genuine misunderstanding apart from conduct that's actually harassment, which means it can just as easily talk someone out of raising something serious as help them resolve something minor. Mend is trained specifically on that distinction, which is also what makes the automatic safety protocols below possible.

Will employees actually use a corporate conflict-resolution tool?

None of this matters if employees don't feel safe using the product, because they fear HR or management is “listening in.”

That's why Mend is built from the ground up with total user anonymity: no persistent record of what's shared. Session content is never truly saved; it's held in volatile memory and flushed as soon as the session ends.

  • A safety net, not a loopholeA fixed, automatic safety protocol: if what's being discussed meets the legal standard for workplace harassment, discrimination under Title VII, or a physical threat, Thimble routes the session straight to HR on its own, so no one has to rely on a judgment call in the moment.
  • Everything else stays off the recordThat one safeguard is what makes the rest of the promise credible: for everyday friction, nothing is saved, nothing reaches HR, and nothing gets back to your manager.

Mend isn't for every company

The deal Mend makes with customers: they're paying for conflict resolution and team alignment, not control of the process. Control is exactly what prevents employees from feeling safe enough to let their guard down and stop performing for an audience.

That's a feature, not a bug: AI's advantage here

  • An AI conflict-resolution facilitator is unbiased and has no stake in the outcome.
  • People are measurably more comfortable opening up to an AI persona because they don't fear judgment.
  • Mend is always available and doesn't pull another person's time into resolving someone else's conflict.

The Research Against “No Friction, No Dissent”

Team dynamics and conflict, from three researchers

  • Patrick Lencioni's “artificial harmony” (The Five Dysfunctions of a Team): teams that avoid conflict end up with unresolved disagreements and unvoiced concerns sitting just under the surface, which resurface later as resentment or disengagement rather than actually going away.
  • Amy Edmondson's psychological safety research (Harvard Business School): teams that prioritize harmony over honest discourse consistently make worse decisions than teams where disagreement is normalized. Psychological safety isn't the absence of conflict, it's the thing that makes productive conflict possible.
  • Francesca Gino's research on constructive disagreement: teams significantly outperform peers when leaders explicitly model and reward disagreement; task conflict generated by even one or two team members has been shown, across multiple studies, to positively predict team performance.

The goal isn't zero conflict, it's conflict that actually gets resolved instead of either suppressed (false harmony) or escalated unmanaged. Mend is a structured path to resolution, which is different from a tool that just makes conflict disappear.
